\section{Scheduling Theory}
\label{sec:preliminaries-related-work:time-theory}
Scheduling is the task of assigning a finite set of timed events to limited resources with restrictions. In our particular case it is the task of assigning tasks (events), with set duration, relative time dependencies to other tasks and locations to days and times (limited resource). \\
Most existing scheduling languages are designed for professional areas like school time tabling~\cite{bib:2017-dmirovic} or sports time tabling~\cite{bib:2022-lester}. We on the other hand want to give the option of using our approach in everyday life. As personal scheduling is a lot more prone to change in comparison to business schedules. We need to ensure that compilation is as fast as possible. To achieve this we decided to put a cap on the latest possible time slot. As most of the time appointments tend to be stacked up towards the near future and become less frequent the further we look into the future. Therefore, this method does not restrict the possible solutions as most of the tasks are handled using it anyway. When one wants to schedule further into the future one can increase this maximum time slot, on the downside one has to deal with worse runtimes.

\subsection{Time representation}
\label{subsec:preliminaries-related-work:time-theory:time-representation}
Representing time in a computer can turn out to be quite challenging. While one has to handle Dates, times, time zones and possibly summer and winter time, the representation of time should still be efficient. When one does want to handle instances of this kind, one has to have enough expressivity to specifically define every possible point in time or time span. This is in conflict with the basic \nameref{sec:introduction:motivation} of this thesis. We want to ensure utmost expressivity, while also ensuring a minimal language and compatibility with constraint solving. Hence, instead of making the language hardly solvable with a lot of constraints, we decided to omit this representation of time from the language. \\
Instead, a time slot approach will be used. One time slot is just a discretized unit of time like for example 15 minutes or 10 seconds. Counted from the beginning of solving - starting with zero - relative from the beginning of solving. So, instead of compiling dates ourselves we rely on receiving time slots relative from the point in time of conversion. To clarify we construct the following example: \\
Task A should be scheduled on December the 31st at 18:00 and takes 1 hour. When we are compiling this exactly at December 31st at 17:00 and one time slot is discretized to 15 minutes, we get conclude this task should be scheduled at time slot 4, 5, 6 and 7

When using this approach, we handle only the time of interest (the future), while ensuring the concept of time is discretized and abstracted away as early as possible in the conversion process of natural language to constraints. \\
The issue of summer and winter time will be omitted for simplicity, and we suggest that one uses the actual time the task is assigned to without taking into account the change of time when we either change the time from summer to winter or from winter to summer.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{subsec:preliminaries-related-work:time-theory:time-representation:cost}
During the course of explain our approach, we will mention the idea of cost several times. We define cost as a metric of how preferable a specific solution for a problem is, compared to another one. This means, a solution A with cost of $15$ is superior over a solution B with cost $200$. Theoretically, a cost of $0$ is the best possible solution for this problem. In practice, such a solution will hardly ever be achievable. We will give further explanations in addition to an exact formal definition on this topic through the course of \autoref{chapter:formal-framework}. Using cost prefers solutions with scheduled tasks over unscheduled ones. This allows us to handle such constraints without rendering the whole solution invalid.

\subsection{Hard vs. Soft Time Constraints}
The notion of hard and soft time constraints are both covered by the \ac{IR}. When we mention hard time constraints in this thesis, we both mean hard as in timely restricted, at a specific time and for a specific amount of time. And hard as in non-negotiable - it must be scheduled validly. \\
On the other hand, when talking about soft time constraints, we tackle both tasks, which can be scheduled in a broader range of time, with possibly differing durations (not part of this thesis). Additionally, soft time constraints handle the problem of tasks one might like to schedule, but it is not required. Hence, we introduce a notion of cost explained in \autoref{subsec:preliminaries-related-work:time-theory:time-representation:cost}.

\subsection{Constraints}
As we are tackling a personal scheduler, we need to cover the most common requirements, one might need to define in such everyday scenarios. Therefore, we identified the following basic requirements:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Duration, to define how long a task will take.
    \item Possible assignments, to define at what point in time the task may be scheduled.
    \item Dependencies, to depict that one task has to be scheduled after the other.
    \item Locations, to emphasize efficiency and reduce unnecessary traveling.
\end{itemize}
All of the above will be explained in detail in \autoref{chapter:methodology-language-design}.

\section{Superstratum Language}
For ease of use we decided against defining our own language from scratch. Therefore, we use \Ac{JSON} to represent our basic language. The decision fell on this format, as it is widely spread and easily human-readable. If one wanted to instead use \Ac{XML}, it can be converted to \ac{JSON} and back~\cite{bib:2017-LaFontaine}.

\section{Existing Scheduling Languages and Standards}
After explaining the basic idea of the \ac{IR}, we need to clarify which formats and standards do already exist and why our language is still solving problems, the other languages can not. One of the most widely used formats is the \textbf{iCalendar} format proposed as a standard in RFC 5545~\cite{bib:rfc5545}. It defines a set of rules for defining text based, possibly reoccurring, tasks. It is used in both professional and personal scheduling applications. As this language has a lot of rules it is also more expressive than the language (or set of constraints) we offer as a solution. The problem with the iCalendar format is that it will not be easily convertible to be given to a solver. Another format is defined by the \Ac{ITC}~\cite{bib:itc2019format}. It serves the purpose of defining a problem, which tries to assign courses to a dedicated room at a dedicated time. Similar to our approach it uses discretized time slots for scheduling. Again, like iCalendar before, it is too complex for the task at hand, as we only have one resource, which is the person allocating the tasks themselves, while the proposed format tackles at least two constraint resources at once. Meaning, we need fewer dimensions in comparison to the format proposed by the \ac{ITC}.
